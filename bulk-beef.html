Where a share wins, and wins big

  • Bone-in ribeye The most expensive steak in the animal, at the same rate as everything else. This one cut is why most people do it.
  • New York strip Same story, and about six pounds of it in a quarter.
  • Standing rib roast The Christmas cut, without the Christmas price.
  • Brisket and short ribs Whole, untrimmed, and yours to cut how you like.

Where it is ordinary, or worse

  • Ground beef Already our cheapest product by the pound. In a share it is about the same. It is also the largest single part of what you take home.
  • Soup and marrow bones Genuinely cheaper bought singly. Ask us to leave them out of your cut sheet if you would rather not pay share rate for them.
  • Stew beef and shank A modest gain at best.

Averaged across a whole quarter, a share works out well ahead of buying the same cuts one at a time, and the more steak you ask for the further ahead it gets. Ring the farm store and we will do the arithmetic with you on the cut sheet you actually want.

How to ask for a share
What does hanging weight actually mean?

Hanging weight is what the carcass weighs after the hide, head and organs are removed, before it is cut and boned. It is the industry standard for pricing bulk beef, and it is what your share is priced on.

Take-home weight is what you actually carry away, once the bones and trim are gone. It is around 65 percent of hanging weight, which is why we show both.

Why is the final price only an estimate?

Because no two animals weigh the same. We know the price per hanging pound exactly, but we do not know your animal's hanging weight until it has hung. The estimates on this page come from the average of the last two years, and in practice the final figure lands within about eight percent.

Do I have to pay the butcher separately?

No. A lot of farms quote a low price per pound and then leave you to settle a second bill at the processor. Our totals include the processing, the fourteen days of dry ageing, the vacuum packing and the labelling. One bill, from us.

Can I split a share with somebody else?

Yes, and most people do. Mention it when you ring and we will pack it as two matching sets, so there is nothing to divide up on the driveway. Whoever arranges it settles with us, and the two of you sort it out privately.

What if I have no idea how I want it cut?

Leave the cut sheet exactly as it comes. Every option has a default already chosen, and the defaults are what most households ask for. If you would rather talk it through, ring the farm store and we will fill it in with you.

How much freezer space do I really need?

About one cubic foot for every 27 pounds of packed beef. A quarter needs roughly 4 cubic feet, a half about 8, a whole animal about 16. There is a fuller breakdown further up this page.

How long will it keep?

Vacuum-sealed steaks and roasts hold their quality for about a year in a freezer that stays at 0°F. Ground beef is best inside six months. Nothing becomes unsafe after that, but the texture begins to suffer.

When is the next animal ready?

We take shares from a small number of animals each month rather than all at once, so there is usually something within four to six weeks. Ring the farm store and we will tell you what is coming ready. Everything you need before you call is further down this page.

Can you deliver a whole share?

A quarter goes out on the normal Friday round across Ann Arbor, Ypsilanti, Saline, Dexter and Chelsea. Halves and whole animals are collection only, simply because of the volume. Bring a cooler or two.

What if my freezer is not ready in time?

We can hold a share frozen at the farm for up to three weeks after processing at no charge. Mention it when you arrange the share, or ring the store once you know.

What a share actually is

A share is the oldest way to buy beef and still the cheapest. You buy part of a specific animal, you decide how it is cut, and you pay one price for every pound of it, whether that pound is ribeye or ground. It is how families around Ann Arbor bought beef for a hundred years before supermarkets made it feel unusual.

The beef in these shares comes from our own herd on 112 acres on Wagner Road. The animals are born here, raised outdoors on grass, and finished on grass rather than grain. They are never given hormones or antibiotics, and no synthetic herbicide, pesticide or fertiliser touches the ground they graze.

Quarter, half or whole

A quarter suits two adults who cook most nights, or a family of four eating beef two or three times a week. It is roughly 110 pounds of packed meat and needs about four cubic feet of freezer. A half is the size most people settle on, often split between two households. A whole animal is the cheapest per pound and usually goes to three or four families together.

If none of those fit, the single cuts and the mixed bundle boxes are there instead, with no freezer commitment at all.

How a share is priced

Shares are priced on hanging weight, which is what the carcass weighs once the hide, head and organs are off, before it is cut and boned. You pay one rate for every pound of it, whether that pound comes back as ribeye or as ground. The rate covers the animal, the USDA processing, the fourteen days of ageing, the vacuum packing and the labelling, so there is no second bill from the butcher.

Because no two animals weigh the same, the final figure is only known once your animal has hung. We will give you the current rate and a realistic range on the telephone before you commit to anything.

On the cheapest cuts, soup bones in particular, a share is slightly worse value. We would rather say so than have you work it out afterwards.
